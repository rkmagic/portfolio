## What are they?

You see them every day on your device. An average user receives more than 30-40 notifications per day. On Android, notifications sit in the tray most people picture, but product teams often treat a whole family of surfaces as one toolkit: standard push, sticky (persistent) notifications that stay in the tray until dismissed, and FSI (full-screen intent) for high-stakes moments. I've had the chance to experiment with all the notification options for the Android device and this article is a reflection of my learnings.

Android has been tightening notification rules for a while. Since Android 8 (Oreo), every notification belongs to a channel, and users can mute individual channels instead of the whole app. Since Android 13, apps must request runtime permission (`POST_NOTIFICATIONS`) before sending anything. That is a win for users and a challenge for product teams. The permission prompt can only be shown once if the user denies it, so timing matters. Opt-in rates on Android vary from 60% to 80% (much lower on iOS) and depend on app category: fintech, social, travel, entertainment, and so on.

## Types of notifications

Broadly, notifications can be split by type or by technical behavior:

Type:
- Transactional - a payment went through, a message arrived, a follow request. Users tolerate interruption here because the value is unambiguous and time-sensitive.
- Content / engagement - breaking news, a horoscope reading, a game nudge, a discount, a reminder from your meditation app. These can bring users back, but too many lead to irritation and notification fatigue.
- Personalized - built on content, these rely on user activity: picking up where you left off in a movie, continuing a game, or finishing onboarding. They can be powerful, but need ML-driven backend segmentation.

Technical:
- Sticky notifications and full-screen intents are not "a notification" in the strict sense, but function as one.
- A sticky notification sits permanently in the tray (high accidental-click surface) and sometimes cannot be dismissed by swiping or clearing. Dismiss buttons need to be built, or users may turn off notifications for the app entirely.
- Full-screen notifications take over the whole screen and are the most intrusive tool in the kit, reserved for rare, high-value moments. Android usually reserves them for apps like the clock or other system apps, with tighter rules on how they can be used.

## How to get the best out of them?

Products continue to optimize notifications and get the most from this channel despite competition. Duolingo is a strong example of using notifications to bring users back. Another app I like is Swiggy, the Indian food delivery app, which nudges users with their favorite foods at specific times of day.

**Be smart about the permission.** If you cannot get permission, your strategy does not matter. The gate has to open, and you do not get many chances. Showing the prompt at a positive moment in the experience helps. Even if the user denies it, you can still nudge them to change it in settings.

![Android notification permission](/images/android-permission.png)

**Cohort, don't broadcast.** The same notification sent to everyone burns out fast. Power users can absorb more volume; dormant users need less, more relevant volume. Undifferentiated sends are the biggest driver of fatigue. Content type matters too: one user may want sports updates but not weather. AI-driven personalization is now close to standard. Personalization can apply across time of day, day of week, onboarding preferences, and more.

**Check the integration end-to-end.** Delivery rate shows how many users actually received your notifications. Integration issues can drag it down: the push ID may not register correctly, or the notification SDK may fail to initialize. Broken deeplinks also hurt UX. Notifications behave differently depending on the OS (even custom Android OS) and OEM, so a thorough review across devices is a must.

**Win the tray, not just the send.** Notifications compete with every other app for the same space. Small design changes move clicks: a clear CTA, images when useful, sharper copy, A/B tests across cohorts can improve CTRs incrementally. Even without a click, view-through attribution can still win the user. Sticky notifications work well for real-time use cases like tracking an Uber driver or counting down to a ticket release. Do not use deceptive copy or spam users; once trust breaks, it is hard to recover.

**Notifications aligned with your customer journey.** Landing users on the right screen can move core metrics. A notification to buy a product only helps if it opens the right page. Notifications can also be spread across the lifecycle: D0, D3, D7, D14, where D0 is install day.

**Plan for decay.** Every notification type follows a novelty curve: CTR is highest early and drops from there. Treat that decay as expected, not as proof the format failed, and build refresh cycles instead of chasing early numbers forever.

## Takeaway

Notifications are still useful. In a world of low attention spans and many apps competing for the same click, a smart strategy across integration, design, and execution can drive meaningful traffic from a crowded but valuable channel.
