No matter the business, you will likely work with corporate clients at some point, even if your product is used directly by retail customers. Sometimes you grow by selling B2B, or by bundling your product with other business offerings. Managing complex relationships across stakeholders can forge strong partnerships that pay off over time. Here is what I learned:

![Clients work](/images/clients.jpg)

1. <b>Understanding what the client is actually optimizing for</b>. Every client has a stated reason for working with you and an internal one. The stated reason is usually revenue or a capability they lack. The internal one is whatever their counterpart is measured on, and whatever risk your product introduces on their side. That risk is real and rarely discussed openly. If you know what it is, much of their behavior stops looking like obstruction and starts looking like self-preservation. It also lets you pre-empt it instead of reacting six months later. Expectation management is a major part of client work.
2. <b>Aligning on scope before you start</b>. Document the scope of delivery and agree on it upfront. Ambiguity causes trouble later. Wasted effort is worse than a hard conversation early. It is bad for the company and for you personally, because there is nothing to show for it. Wins, even small consistent ones, are what you want.
3. <b>Being available and responsive</b>. This may sound unpopular, but I believe it builds trust. If a client sends an email or calls, respond immediately or at least within a few hours the same day. Even if you do not know the answer, let them know you are on top of it. They feel comfortable and get the impression that they matter to you. I often see people take days to respond because they have not found an answer yet. The other side has no idea whether you are doing anything about it.
4. <b>Blame games on large forums</b>. I follow this with clients and internal teams alike. If there is a mistake or problem and there is reasonable time to fix it without damaging business goals, follow up with the individual responsible rather than escalating in a big thread. The other side feels attacked when you make it public, and you still have to work with them. Maintaining relationships in hard times is painful, but in the long run it pays off for you and your business.
5. <b>Delivering what you promised</b>. It is better to underpromise than overpromise. Over time, failure to deliver erodes credibility until your words no longer carry weight. Ambitions should be high, but also realistic.
6. <b>Nurturing internal champions</b>. Large clients have owners attached to your business who vouch for you internally. They need to be taken care of. Deliver on goals, show progress, and arm them with the right data so they can sell you. Too much data can also leave you vulnerable, so be deliberate about which numbers go out and at what level of detail: north star metrics, quarter-on-quarter progress, outcomes.
7. <b>End on a high note</b>. Not all partnerships work out, and that is okay. Still, I think it is important to close them on a good note. Psychology's peak-end rule says people remember how an experience ended more strongly than the average. Burning bridges makes little sense unless staying with the client is unlawful or unethical. Opportunities can arise later, and these relationships can help you capitalize on them.
8. <b>Constant communication</b>. Progress updates, sharing what you have built, metric reports: these should go to clients regularly. Even when there is no progress, stay in touch. Ask for feedback, learn what challenges they face in the relationship, and see whether you can solve them. What worked for us was giving this structure instead of leaving it to goodwill: regular catchups, QBRs, deep dives, and walkthroughs, each with its own cadence and purpose. Cadence beats volume. It shows they are heard. They are paying for your product. That is the least they deserve. Some communication is always better than none.
9. <b>Socializing jargon</b>. Your company may use jargon the client never understands. Avoid it when you can. If it is necessary, explain it early so everyone is aligned. Jargon throws off clients and makes them feel uncomfortable. They do not understand what you are saying and may feel foolish. Sometimes people ask, but time gets wasted translating it, and then they have to remember it every time.
10. <b>Being smart in saying no</b>. Clients often suggest product changes or customizations that do not fit your strategy. You do not have to say no directly. Reframe it as something you will consider, but explain that the current priority is elsewhere. You are the experts at what you do. Being receptive without shutting them down is a better way to handle requests you cannot fulfill. It is also useful to get an outside lens on the product.
