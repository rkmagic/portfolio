No matter which business, I believe, you will at some point end up working with clients who are corporates/businesses, even though your product is directly used by retail customers. Sometimes you are growing the business by selling B2B, or finding new avenues of revenue by bundling your product with B2B products. Managing complex relationships across stakeholders can go a long way in forging a formidable parternship that can yield dividends for your company. Here's what I learnt from my experience:

![Clients work](/images/clients.jpg)

1. <b>Understanding what the client is actually optimizing for</b>. Every client has a stated reason for working with you and an internal one. The stated reason is usually revenue or some capability they don't have. The internal one is whatever their counterpart is being measured on, and whatever risk your product introduces on their side. That risk is real and it is rarely discussed openly. If you know what it is, a lot of their behaviour stops looking like obstruction and starts looking like self-preservation. It also lets you pre-empt it instead of reacting to it six months in. Expectation management is a major part of working with clients.
2. <b>Aligning on scope before you start</b>. Its highly important to document the scope of delivery to the client and mutually agree on it. Ambiguity with scope can cause troubles down the road. Wasted effort is worse than a hard conversation upfront. It's bad for the company and it's bad for you personally - there's nothing to show for it. Wins, even small consistent ones, are what you want.
3. <b>Being available and responsive</b>. Now this might sound unpopular but I believe doing so builds trust. If there is an email sent by a client or a phone call, respond immediately or at least within a few hours of the same day. Even if you don't know the answer, its still important to let them know that you are on top of it. They feel comfortable and gives them the impression that they are important to you. I sometimes find people taking days to respond just because they are yet to find an answer. The other side has no idea if you are doing anything about it.
4. <b>Blame games on large forums</b>. This is something that I follow, not just with clients but even with internal teams. If there is a mistake or a problem and there is reasonable time to fix it without damaging business goals, I believe its better to follow up with the individual responsible rather than escalating it in a big thread. The other side feels attacked if you make it big, and you still have to work with them. Its not like they'll disappear. Maintaining relationships in the hard times can be painful but in the long run, its fruitful to you and your business.
5. <b>Delivering what you promised</b>. Its always better to underpromise than overpromise. Over time, failure to deliver can build up and credibility is lost. Your words no more hold meaning. Ambitions of course need to be set high but also realistic enough.
6. <b>Nurturing internal champions</b>. Large clients have owners attached to your business who internally vouch for you in their companies. They need to be well taken care of. Deliver the goals, show progress and arm them with the right data so that they can sell you. Of coures, too much data is also not good as it can leave you in a vulnerable position. You need a deliberate view on which numbers go out and at what level of detail - north star metrics, quarter-on-quarter progress, outcomes.
7. <b>End on a high note</b>. Not all partnerships work out and its okay to end them. However, I feel its important to bring it to closure on a good note. Pyschology teaches us peak-end rule where people remember how an experience ended more strongly than the total average. Burning bridges makes no sense unless its unlawful or unethical to be with that client. Opportunites can arise in the future and these relationships can be useful to capitalize on them.
8. <b>Constant Communication</b>. Progress updates, sharing what you've built, metric reports - these are some examples of the things that need to be shared with clients periodically. Even if there is no progress, talk to clients regularly. See if they have any feedback, any challenges that they currently face with the relationship and if you can solve it. What worked for us was giving this an actual structure rather than leaving it to goodwill: regular catchups, QBRs, deep dives and walkthroughs, each with its own cadence and purpose. Cadence beats volume. It shows that they are heard. They are paying for your product. That's the least they deserve. Some communication is always better than no communication.
9. <b>Socializing jargon</b>. Your company might be using a bunch of jargon which the client never understands. Its important to avoid them. If its absolutely necessary, then its better to explain them right at the beginning so that everyone is on the same page. Using jargon throws off clients and makes them feel uncomfortable. They don't understand what you're saying and they feel dumb or foolish. Sometimes people ask, but time gets wasted in trying to translate the jargon and now they have to remember it every time.
10. <b>Being smart in saying no</b>. Often, clients can suggest changes to your product or customizations which may not align with your strategy. You don't have to say no directly. You can always reframe it as something that you'll consider but the priority for now is so and so. You are experts at what you do. Being receptive and not shutting them down is a nicer way to handle requests you can't fulfill due to xyz reasons. Its always good to get a picture from an outside lens.
