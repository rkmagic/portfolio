## Why widgets

Widgets are small elements you can place on your phone home screen. Most devices come with 1-2 widgets by default, usually the weather widget or Google Search on Android. They extend the app and create another entry surface alongside the app icon and notifications. Having built widgets as the main product, I can share firsthand experience of the challenges and opportunities.

It is hard to get users to add widgets unless they come preloaded on the device, and adoption is often below 10-15% depending on the product. Still, they can drive repeat app opens by removing the need to hunt for the app icon. They also work as an indirect channel: users may notice useful or interesting content on the widget, then open the app later. Widgets are a window into your core features, so their intent should be designed carefully, with equal attention to function and aesthetics.

## Things to take care of

- **Stay small unless necessary.** A widget that tries to be a mini-app can backfire. Smaller footprints are easier to tolerate. The larger the widget, the more friction there is to place it, since it takes up more space, so the function must justify the size. Phones also handle widget grids differently, such as 4x4 or 3x4, so design for those constraints and avoid wasted space.

- **Give it its own interaction model.** A widget that only displays information is underused unless it serves a utility case like a calendar. Widgets with controls such as refresh, next/previous, or a settings shortcut treat the widget as a real surface, not a shrunk screenshot of the app. 

- **One tap, one destination.** Ambiguity is the enemy on a surface this small. A tap should lead to one clear destination, with no "which of these three things did you mean" moment after the user lands. On one widget we shipped, moving from multiple content pieces to a single piece at a time, similar to a flipboard, which raised widget clicks by 40%.

- **Brand it as its own asset, not a scaled-down icon.** Widget imagery, the Android add prompt, and the widget's visual identity need their own design pass. They do not inherit well from the app icon or splash screen.

- **The widget must stay in sync with the app.** A widget has no scroll context and no "above the fold" lead-in from the rest of the app. Whatever hook or prompt it shows has to work as a complete, self-contained thought. Its content also needs to stay synced with the app. If I set preferences in the app and the widget does not reflect them, or vice versa, the experience breaks down.

- **Smart prompts.** Widgets have to be added by the user. The default add flow often places them on a separate screen that may be 3 or 4 swipes away from the main home screen. Drag and drop is better because it lets users place the widget where they want, but it also takes more effort. - **Technical challenges need workarounds.** Android widgets render through `RemoteViews`, not arbitrary custom layouts, so designers need to work within a constrained component set from the start instead of adapting designs later. Google also puts a practical floor on how often widget content can refresh on its own, roughly every 30 minutes. Anything faster needs `WorkManager` or an alarm-driven update, which introduces battery tradeoffs. Since Android 12, widgets are also expected to adopt the system's dynamic Material You colors and support flexible resizing. A widget with a fixed palette or rigid size increasingly feels out of place next to system widgets. You also cannot track CTRs directly because widget impressions are not accessible to app developers, so performance has to be measured more like a regular app icon.

## Takeaway

Widgets are powerful triggers for product usage, and the act of adding one already signals high intent and stickiness. Used alongside notifications, they can improve retention and strengthen the user's connection with your product.

Even if only a small percentage of users add a widget, they are likely your power users and often the voice of the product. Treat them well :).
